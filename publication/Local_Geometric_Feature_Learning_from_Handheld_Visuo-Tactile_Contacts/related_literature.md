# Related Literature

Vision-based tactile sensing has shifted tactile perception from sparse force measurement toward image-based local geometry estimation. GelSight demonstrates this shift by using optical measurements of elastomer deformation to recover high-resolution contact geometry and related force cues (Yuan et al., 2017). DIGIT extends the same design logic toward compact, low-cost, and reproducible sensing hardware suitable for robot hands and learning-based manipulation experiments (Lambeta et al., 2020). From a representation-learning perspective, this makes tactile images comparable to other partial geometric observations: 3DMatch shows that learned local descriptors can improve matching under noisy, incomplete RGB-D scans (Zeng et al., 2017), but tactile contacts introduce a stricter locality because each observation is physically limited to the sensor-object contact patch.

Visuo-tactile reconstruction methods treat this locality as a source of complementary geometric evidence. In multimodal shape reconstruction, vision supplies global object context while touch improves local detail near contact regions (Smith et al., 2020). ShapeMap 3-D formalizes this complementarity through incremental mapping, where GelSight-derived local shape measurements refine a depth-based object model (Suresh et al., 2022). TouchSDF pushes the tactile pathway further by predicting local surface meshes from tactile images before fitting an implicit signed-distance representation (Comi et al., 2023). VTacO similarly uses tactile observations to improve in-hand reconstruction of rigid and deformable objects when visual evidence is limited by contact and occlusion (Xu et al., 2023). These studies establish that tactile images contain learnable geometric structure, but their outputs are object reconstructions rather than interpretable local feature labels.

Recent tactile representation-learning work reduces dependence on manually engineered perception pipelines, but it often prioritizes transfer or control performance over semantic interpretability. Sparsh learns general tactile representations through self-supervised pretraining across large tactile image collections, addressing the cost of task-specific labels and sensor-specific models (Higuera et al., 2024). 3D-ViTac fuses tactile and visual data into a unified 3D representation for fine-grained bimanual manipulation, preserving spatial relationships that are useful for diffusion-policy learning (Huang et al., 2025). TacThru-UMI combines simultaneous tactile-visual sensing with imitation learning, showing that tactile feedback can improve manipulation success where vision-only policies degrade under occlusion or precision contact requirements (Li et al., 2026). These approaches show the value of learned tactile features, but they leave open how a single pose-free contact should be mapped to an explicit geometric category useful for shape reasoning.

Handheld and robot-free systems address a different bottleneck: the difficulty of collecting diverse contact-rich data with fixed robot platforms. UMI uses a sensorized handheld gripper to collect demonstrations that can be transferred to robot policies, emphasizing action representation and deployment across robot embodiments (Chi et al., 2024). Touch in the Wild adds tactile sensing to a portable gripper and learns cross-modal representations that attend to physically relevant contact regions during manipulation (Zhu et al., 2025). FreeTacMan moves further toward human-centric data collection through a wearable visuo-tactile gripper designed for intuitive robot-free demonstrations (Wu et al., 2026). MimicTouch similarly uses human tactile demonstrations to learn tactile-guided manipulation strategies and then bridges the embodiment gap with residual reinforcement learning (Yu et al., 2025). These systems support the practicality of handheld visuo-tactile acquisition, but their evaluation targets manipulation policies rather than local geometric feature recognition.

Across these lines of work, tactile sensing is consistently treated as a source of dense local information, yet the role of local geometry is usually absorbed into reconstruction, broad representation learning, or downstream control. Reconstruction methods require spatial integration to convert contacts into object-level shape estimates (Suresh et al., 2022; Comi et al., 2023), while policy-learning systems evaluate whether tactile signals improve task success rather than whether individual contacts have interpretable geometric meaning (Huang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). This leaves a specific gap for primitive shape recognition from pose-free contacts: before multiple touches can be accumulated, each tactile image must first be converted into a reliable local geometric observation. The present study addresses that gap by learning a CNN-based local feature encoder for handheld visuo-tactile contacts and evaluating whether its predictions provide a transparent intermediate representation for primitive-shape inference.
